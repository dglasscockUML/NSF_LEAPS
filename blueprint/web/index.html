Some basic topology theorems we use over and over:

  • IsClosedMap.closure_image_eq_of_continuous shows that \(\overline{fA} = f\overline{A}\) as long as \(f: X \to Y\) is a closed map

  • IsCompact.image the continuous image of a compact set is compact

  • IsProperMap.isClosedMap shows that a proper map is closed

  • Continuous.isProperMap shows that a continuous function from a compact space to a T2 space is proper.

  • mem_closure_iff: a point \(x\) is in \(\overline{A}\) if and only if for all open \(U \ni x\), \(U \cap \overline{A} \neq \emptyset \).

  • IsClosed.isCompact: a closed subset of a compact space is compact

  • IsCompact.isClosed: in a Hausdorff space, a compact set is closed

  • isClosed_closure: the closure of a set is closed

Lemma 7
#
Lean Remarks
#

quotientByCERIsCompactHausdorff Let \(X\) be a compact Hausdorff space, and let \(R \subseteq X^2\) be a closed equivalence relation. The quotient \(X/R\), endowed with the quotient topology, is a compact Hausdorff space.

Proof ▶

Since the quotient map \(\pi : X \to X/R\) is continuous and \(X\) is compact, we have that \(X/R\) is compact. To see that \(X/R\) is Hausdorff, let \(y, z \in X/R\) be distinct. Since \(R\) is closed, the sets \(\pi ^{-1}\{ y\} \) and \(\pi ^{-1}\{ z\} \) are disjoint closed subsets of \(X\). Because \(X\) is compact Hausdorff, it is a normal space. Thus there exist disjoint open sets \(U, V \subseteq X\) such that \(\pi ^{-1}\{ y\} \subseteq U\) and \(\pi ^{-1}\{ z\} \subseteq V\).

Let \(\widetilde{U} = \pi (U^c)^c\) and \(\widetilde{V} = \pi (V^c)^c\). It is easy to see that \(\widetilde{U} \cap \widetilde{V} = \emptyset \), that \(y \in \widetilde{U}\), and that \(z \in \widetilde{V}\). It remains to show \(\widetilde{U}\) and \(\widetilde{V}\) are open. For this, it suffices to show \(\pi \) is a closed map, but it is a standard fact that continuous maps of compact Hausdorff spaces are closed.

nbhdOfDiagForcesOtherSetContainment Let \(X\) be a compact Hausdorff space. Let \(y \in X\) and \(U \subseteq X\) be open and contain \(y\). 0.3.1 Definitions

Let \(S\) be a set.

Definition 13 Collection
#

A collection of subsets of \(S\) is an element of \(\collections {S}\).

Definition 14 Nonempty collection
#

A collection \(\mathcal{F}\in \text{Fam}{S}\) is nonempty if \(\mathcal{F}\neq \emptyset \).

Definition 15 Proper collection
#

A collection \(\mathcal{F}\in \text{Fam}{S}\) is proper if \(\mathcal{F}\neq \powerset {S}\).

Definition 16 Upward closed collection
#

A collection of sets \(\mathcal{F}\in \collections {S}\) is upward closed if for all \(A, B \subseteq S\), ((\(A \in \mathcal{F}\) and \(A \subseteq B\)) implies \(B \in \mathcal{F}\)).

Definition 17 Family of subsets of \(S\)
#

A collection of sets \(\mathcal{F}\in \collections {S}\) is a family if it is upward closed. We denote the set of families on \(S\) by \(\text{Fam}{S}\).

Definition 18 Partition regular family
#

A family \(\mathcal{F}\in \text{Fam}{S}\) is partition regular family if for all \(A, B \subseteq S\), \(A \cup B \in \mathcal{F}\) implies \(A \in \mathcal{F}\) or \(B \in \mathcal{F}\).

Definition 19 Filter family
#

A family \(\mathcal{F}\in \text{Fam}{S}\) is a filter family if for all \(A, B \subseteq S\), \(A \in \mathcal{F}\) and \(B \in \mathcal{F}\) implies \(A \cap B \in \mathcal{F}\).

Definition 20 Ultrafilter family
#

A family \(\mathcal{F}\in \text{Fam}{S}\) is an ultrafilter family if it is both a partition regular family and a filter family.

0.3.2 Set lattice

  1. \(\cup : \text{Fam}{S}^2 \to \text{Fam}{S}\)

  2. \(\cap : \text{Fam}{S}^2 \to \text{Fam}{S}\)

0.3.3 Dual families

Definition 21 Dual map
#

The dual map \(*: \mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(S)) \to \mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(S))\) is defined by

\[ \mathcal{F}^* = \{ A \subseteq S : \forall B \in \mathcal{F}, \ A \cap B \neq \emptyset \} . \]

For \(\mathcal{F}\in \collections {S}\), its image \(\mathcal{F}^*\) is the dual of \(\mathcal{F}\).

Facts on the level of collections:

  1. \(*\) is antitone: for collections \(\mathcal{F}\) and \(\mathcal{G}\), if \(\mathcal{F}\subseteq \mathcal{G}\), then \(\mathcal{G}^* \subseteq \mathcal{F}^*\)

    • Proof: theorem 28

  2. For all collections \(\mathcal{F}\) and \(\mathcal{G}\), \((\mathcal{F}\cup \mathcal{G})^* = \mathcal{F}^* \cap \mathcal{G}^*\) and \((\mathcal{F}\cap \mathcal{G})^* = \mathcal{F}^* \cup \mathcal{G}^*\)

    • Proof: theorem 30 and theorem 31

Facts on the level of families:

  1. \(*\) maps families to families, that is, \(*: \text{Fam}{S} \to \text{Fam}{S}\)

    • Proof: theorem 24

    • Note that the edge cases \(\emptyset ^* = \mathcal{P}(S)\) and \(\mathcal{P}(S)^* = \emptyset \) hold by strict interpretation of the definition. Thus, in the theorems proving facts about the dual, there are no separate edge cases to consider.

    • There is a more general statement here we might favor: \(*\) takes \(\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(S))\) to \(\text{Fam}{S}\).

  2. Useful equivalent formulation: for all families \(\mathcal{F}\),

    \[ \mathcal{F}^* = \{ A \subseteq S : S \setminus A \not\in \mathcal{F}\} \]

    • Proof: theorem 25

  3. \(*\) is an involution on families: for all families \(\mathcal{F}\), \((\mathcal{F}^*)^* = \mathcal{F}\)

    • Proof: theorem 26

    • There is a more general statement we might favor: \((\mathcal{F}^*)^*\) is the upward-closure of \(\mathcal{F}\).

  4. \(*: \text{Fam}{S} \to \text{Fam}{S}\) is a bijection

    • Proof: theorem 27

  5. For all families \(\mathcal{F}\), \(\mathcal{F}\) is partition regular if and only if \(\mathcal{F}^*\) is a filter

    • Proof: theorem 29

Theorem 32
#

If \(\mathcal{F}\) and \(\mathcal{G}\) are families, then \(\mathcal{F}\curlyvee \mathcal{G}\) is a family.

Proof ▶

Daniel says: Updated! By definition, \(\mathcal{F}\curlyvee \mathcal{G}\) is a collection of subsets of \(S\). We need to show that it is upward closed.

Let \(A \in \mathcal{F}\curlyvee \mathcal{G}\) and \(A \subseteq A' \subseteq S\). We must show that \(A' \in \mathcal{F}\curlyvee \mathcal{G}\). By definition, there exists \(B \in \mathcal{F}\) and \(C \in \mathcal{G}\) such that \(A = B \cap C\). Define \(B' = B \cup A'\) and \(C' = C \cup A'\).

We claim that

  1. \(B' \in \mathcal{F}\),

  2. \(C' \in \mathcal{G}\),

  3. \(B' \cap C' = A'\).

If we show all three, then we are done, since it will follow that \(A' \in \mathcal{F}\curlyvee \mathcal{G}\).

(1) Since \(B \in \mathcal{F}\), \(\mathcal{F}\) is upward closed, and \(B \subseteq B' \subseteq S\), we have that \(B' \in \mathcal{F}\).

(2) Since \(C \in \mathcal{G}\), \(\mathcal{G}\) is upward closed, and \(C \subseteq C' \subseteq S\), we have that \(C' \in \mathcal{G}\).

(3) From set theory, union distributes over intersection (\(X \cup (Y \cap Z) = (X \cup Y) \cap (X \cup Z)\)). We see

\[ B' \cap C' = (B \cup A') \cap (C \cup A') = (B \cap C) \cup A' = A \cup A'. \]

Since \(A \subseteq A'\), \(A \cup A' = A'\), as desired.

0.4.1 Definitions

The topological dynamical systems in Mathlib are called Flows. See https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/Dynamics/Flow.html. A flow is an action of a topological monoid on a topological space by continuous maps. Since we care only about discrete semigroups (monoids), we are likely better off defining our own class of topological dynamical systems. We may still find it useful to use the notion of invariance, here: https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/Dynamics/Flow.html

Question: Do we define topological dynamical systems as semigroup actions or monoid actions? The only difference is that a monoid action requires that the identity element of the monoid acts by the identity map on the space. This is a natural assumption, but may not be required for us.

Answer: semigroup actions. It seems we can do everything we need to do while avoiding monoids, and especially monoid actions.

Question: Do we define topological dynamical systems with phase space “compact Hausdorff” or “uniform”? Uniform is more general, but perhaps is not necessary for our purposes.

Answer: compact Hausdorff phase spaces

Question: A “system” for us is usually a bundle of a phase space \(X\) together with an action of a semigroup \(S\). Answer: since the data provided is essentially the same, I don’t right now see a meaningful difference here. Let’s go with the more recognizable “system” as a bundling of space and action.

existsMinimalSubset Let \(S\) be a semigroup and \(X\) be an \(S\)-system. There exists a non-empty, closed, \(S\)-invariant subset \(Z \subseteq X\) (that is, an \(S\)-subsystem \(Z\) of \(X\)) such that the \(S\)-system \(Z\) is minimal.

0.9.2 The Grothendieck group

It is well known that a commutative, cancellative semigroup \(S\) embeds into its group of quotients. The Grothendieck group, \(\gro (S)\), generalizes this by omitting the cancellativity requirement at the cost of downgrading the embedding to a semigroup homomorphism \(S \to \gro (S)\). Since \(\gro (S)\) is typically considered for monoids rather than semigroups, we briefly summarize the construction and properties we need in the following theorem.

Let \(S\) be a commutative semigroup and \(X\) be an \(S\)-system on which \(S\) acts by surjections. The set

\[ W \vcentcolon =\big\{ \varphi : \gro (S) \to X \mid \forall g \in \gro (S), \ \forall s \in S, \ \varphi ((\igro s)g) = s (\varphi (g)) \big\} \subseteq X^{\gro (S)} \]

is a non-empty, closed, \(\gro (S)\)-invariant subset of \(X^{\gro (S)}\). Thus, \(W\) is a \(\gro (S)\)-system, and hence, by way of the homomorphism \(\igro : S \to \gro (S)\), an \(S\)-system. The map \(\pi : W \to X\) defined by

\[ \pi (\varphi ) \vcentcolon =\varphi (e_{\gro (S)}) \]

is a factor map of \(S\)-systems.

Proof ▶

That \(W\) is closed and \(\pi \) is continuous follow easily from the definition of the product topology on \(X^{\gro (S)}\).

To see that \(W\) is \(\gro (S)\)-invariant, for \(\varphi \in W\) and \(h \in \gro (S)\), we must show that \(h \varphi \in W\). We must check that for all \(g \in \gro (S)\) and \(s \in S\), we have

\[ (h \varphi ) ((\igro s)g) = s \big((h \varphi ) (g) \big). \]

Let \(g \in \gro (S)\) and \(s \in S\). We see

\[ (h \varphi ) ((\igro s)g) = \varphi ((\igro s)gh) = s \big(\varphi (gh)\big) = s \big((h \varphi ) (g) \big), \]

as desired.

Now we show \(W\) is non-empty and \(\pi \) is surjective. For \(F \subseteq \gro (S)\) finite, define

\[ W_F = \big\{ \varphi : \gro (S) \to X \mid \forall g \in F, \ \forall s \in S, \ \varphi ((\igro s)g) = s \big(\varphi (g)\big) \big\} \subseteq X^{\gro (S)}. \]

It is clear that \(W = \bigcap _F W_F\), where the intersection is taken over all finite sets \(F \subseteq \gro (S)\). We will show that for all \(x \in X\) and all finite \(F \subseteq \gro (S)\), there exists \(\varphi \in W_F\) so that \(\varphi (e_{\gro (S)}) = x\). This will show that \(W\) is non-empty and that \(\pi \) is surjective. Indeed, for any \(x \in X\),

\[ \big\{ W_F \cap \{ \varphi \in X^{\gro (S)} \mid \varphi (e_{\gro (S)}) = x\} \mid F \subseteq \gro (S) \text{ finite} \big\} \]

will be a family of non-empty, closed sets with the finite intersection property. By compactness, there will be some function in every member of the family, and that function will belong to \(W\) and map to \(x\) under \(\pi \).

Let \(F \subseteq \gro (S)\) be finite and \(x \in X\). Without loss of generality, we can assume \(e_{\gro (S)} \in F\). By theorem 241, there exists \(h \in \gro (S)\) such that \(F \subseteq \igro (S) h\). In particular, there exists \(s \in S\) such that \(\igro (s) h = e_{\gro (S)}\). By our standing assumptions, \(s: X \to X\) is a surjection, so there exists \(y \in X\) such that \(sy = x\).

Define \(\varphi : \gro (S) \to X\) by

\[ \varphi (g) = \begin{cases} ty & \text{ if } \exists t \in S, \ g = \igro (t) h \\ y & \text{ else } \end{cases}. \]

We must verify that \(\varphi \) is well-defined. If there exists \(t, r \in S\) such that \(g = \igro (t) h = \igro (r) h\), then \(\igro (t) = \igro (r)\), and so by theorem 242, \(ty = ry\). Note that \(\varphi (h) = y\). Indeed, consider two cases: A) there is \(t \in S\) such that \(h = \igro (t) h\), and B) there is no such \(t\). In case A, \(\igro (t) = e_{\gro (S)}\), so \(\igro (t) = \igro (t^2)\). Since \(t: X \to X\) is a surjection, there exists \(z \in X\) such that \(tz = y\). By theorem 242, we have that

\[ \varphi (h) = ty = t^2 z = tz = y, \]

as claimed. In case B, by definition, \(\varphi (h) = y\), and we are done.

To see that \(\varphi \in W_F\), suppose \(g \in F\) and \(t \in S\). Write \(g = \igro (r)h\) for some \(r \in S\). By the definition of \(\varphi \), we see that

\[ \varphi (\igro (t) g) = \varphi (\igro (t)\igro (r)h) = \varphi (\igro (tr)h) = (tr)y = t(ry) = t \big(\varphi (\igro (r)h)\big) = t\big(\varphi (g)\big). \]

To see that \(\varphi (e_{\gro (S)}) = x\), we see by the definition of \(\varphi \) that

\[ \varphi (e_{\gro (S)}) = \varphi (\igro (s)h) = sy = x. \]

We now prove the map \(\pi \) intertwines with the action of \(S\). (Recall that \(W\) is an \(S\)-system by the homomorphism \(\igro : S \to \gro (S)\), meaning that \(s \varphi \) is defined as \(\igro (s) \varphi \).) For this, let \(\varphi \in W\) and \(s \in S\). We see

\[ s \big(\pi (\varphi )\big) = s \big(\varphi (e_{\gro (S)})\big) = \varphi (\igro (s) e_{\gro (S)}) = (\igro (s) \varphi )(e_{\gro (S)}) = (s \varphi )(e_{\gro (S)}) = \pi (s \varphi ), \]

as was to be shown.

Theorem 246 Universal property of the natural extension
#

Let \(S\) be a commutative semigroup and \(X\) be an \(S\)-system on which \(S\) acts by surjections. The natural extension \(\pi : W \to X\) is the smallest extension of the \(S\)-system \(X\) on which \(S\) acts by homeomorphisms: if \(\rho : V \to X\) is an extension of \(S\)-systems and \(S\) acts on \(V\) by homeomorphisms, then there exists a factor map \(\xi : V \to W\) of \(S\)-systems so that \(\rho = \pi \circ \xi \). In particular, if \(S\) acts on \(X\) by homeomorphisms, then \(\pi \) is an isomorphism of \(S\)-systems.

Proof ▶

Suppose \(\rho : V \to X\) is a factor map of \(S\)-systems and that \(S\) acts on \(V\) by homeomorphisms. By theorem 243, since \(S\) acts on \(V\) by homeomorphisms, we get an action of \(\gro (S)\) on \(V\) that extends it. Note that this means that for all \(v \in V\) and \(s \in S\), we have

\begin{align} \label{eqn_rho_factor_consequence} s (\rho v) = \rho ( sv) = \rho ( (\igro s) v). \end{align}

Define \(\xi : V \to X^{\gro (S)}\) by

\[ \xi : v \mapsto \big( \xi _v : g \mapsto \rho (g v) \big). \]

We will show that \(\xi \) is continuous, that the image of \(\xi \) is in \(W\), that \(\xi : V \to W\) is a factor map of \(S\)-systems, and that \(\rho = \pi \circ \xi \).

To see that \(\xi \) is continuous, consider a cylinder set of \(X^{\gro (S)}\) of the form

\[ [U]_{g} \vcentcolon =\{ \varphi : \gro (S) \to X \mid \varphi (g) \in U \} , \]

where \(g \in \gro (S)\) and \(U \subseteq X\) is open. It is quick to check that

\[ \xi ^{-1} [U]_g = g^{-1} \rho ^{-1} U, \]

which is an open subset of \(V\). Finite intersections of such cylinders, which generate the topology on \(X^{\gro (S)}\), will therefore have open pre-images under \(\xi \), demonstrating that \(\xi \) is continuous.

Next we show that the image of \(\xi \) is in \(W\). Let \(v \in V\), \(g \in \gro (S)\), and \(s \in S\). We must show

\begin{align*} \xi _v((\igro s) g) = s ( \xi _v(g)). \end{align*}

We see that

\[ \xi _v((\igro s) g) = \rho \big( ((\igro s) g) v\big) = s \big( \rho (gv) \big) = s \big( \xi _v(g) \big), \]

where the first and third equalities follows from the definition of \(\xi _v\) and the second follows from ??.

To see that \(\xi \) surjects onto \(W\), we will show that the image of \(\xi \) is dense in \(W\). This suffices since the image of \(V\) under \(\xi \) is closed, and hence equal to \(W\). Let \(\varphi \in W\) and \(g_1, \ldots , g_k \in \gro (S)\). We will show that there exists \(v \in V\) such that for all \(j\), \(\xi _v(g_j) = \varphi (g_j)\). By theorem 241, there exists \(h \in \gro (S)\) and \(s_1, \ldots , s_k \in S\) such that \(g_j = \igro (s_j) h\). Since \(h: V \to V\) is a homeomorphism and \(\rho : V \to X\) is surjective, there exists \(v \in h^{-1} \rho ^{-1} \{ \varphi (h) \} \). It follows that \(\xi _v(h) = \rho ( hv) = \varphi (h)\). Then

\[ \varphi (g_j) = \varphi ((\igro s_j) h) = s_j \big(\varphi (h)\big) = s_j \big(\xi _v(h)\big) = \xi _v((\igro s_j) h) = \xi _v(g_j), \]

as was to be shown.

We have only left to show that \(\xi \) intertwines the action of \(S\) on \(V\) and \(W\). Let \(v \in V\) and \(s \in S\). We must show that \(\xi _{sv} = s \xi _v\). Let \(g \in \gro (S)\). We see

\begin{gather*} \xi _{sv}(g) = \rho (g(sv)) = \rho ((\igro (s) (gv)) = s \big(\rho (gv)\big) \qquad \qquad \qquad \qquad \qquad \qquad \\ \qquad \qquad \qquad \qquad \qquad \qquad = s \big(\xi _v(g)\big) = \xi _v( (\igro s) g) = \xi _v( g(\igro s)) = (s \xi _v) (g), \end{gather*}

as was to be shown.

It remains to prove if \(S\) acts by homeomorphisms, then \(\pi \) is injective, and hence is an isomorphism of \(S\)-systems. Note that this follows immediately from the universal property. Indeed, the universal property gives that since \(S\) acts on \(X\) via homeomorphisms, we have factor maps \(X \to W \to X\) that compose to give the identity. But this means that \(\pi : W \to X\) is injective.

Theorem 247
#

Let \(S\) be a commutative semigroup, \(X\) be an \(S\)-system on which \(S\) acts by surjections, and \(\pi : W \to X\) be its natural extension. The system \(X\) is a minimal \(S\)-system if and only if the system \(W\) is a minimal \(S\)-system.

Proof ▶

Since \(\pi : W \to X\) is a factor map of \(S\)-systems, if \(W\) is a minimal \(S\)-system, then it is immediate that \(X\) is a minimal \(S\)-system.

Suppose conversely that \(X\) is a minimal \(S\)-system. Let \(\varphi , \psi \in W\), and let \(U \subseteq W\) be an open neighborhood of \(\psi \). We will show that there exists \(t \in S\) such that \(t \varphi \in U\).

By the definition of product topology on \(X^{\gro (S)}\), the set \(U\) contains a “cylinder set” around \(\psi \). This means there exists a finite set \(F \subseteq \gro (S)\) and, for all \(f \in F\), an open neighborhood \(U_f \subseteq X\) of \(\psi (f)\) satisfying the following: for any \(\chi \in W\), if \(\chi (f) \in U_f\) for all \(f \in F\), then \(\chi \in U\).

By theorem 241, there exists \(h \in \gro (S)\) such that for all \(f \in F\), there exists \(s_f \in S\) such that \(f = \igro (s_f) h\). Thus, for all \(f \in F\), we have that

\[ s_f \big(\psi (h)\big) = \psi ( \igro (s_f) h) = \psi (f) \in U_f, \]

whereby \(\psi (h) \in \bigcap _{f \in F} s_f^{-1} U_f\).

By the minimality of \(X\), there exists \(t \in S\) such that \(t\varphi (h) \in \bigcap _{f \in F} s_f^{-1} U_f\); that is \(s_f t \varphi (h) \in U_f\) for all \(f \in F\). Moreover, for \(f \in F\),

\[ s_f t \big(\varphi (h)\big) = \varphi ( \igro (s_f) \igro (t) h) = (t \varphi ) (\igro (s_f) h) = (t \varphi ) (f), \]

whereby \((t \varphi ) (f) \in U_f\). It follows that \(t \varphi \in U\), as was to be shown.

Corollary 248
#

Let \(S\) be a commutative semigroup and \(X\) be a minimal \(S\)-system. The natural extension \(\pi : W \to X\) as described in theorem 245 exists, satisfies the universal property described in theorem 246, and is a minimal \(S\)-system.

Proof ▶

By lemma 89, the semigroup \(S\) acts by surjections. Therefore, the hypotheses required for theorem 245, theorem 246, and theorem 247 are met.

Theorem 249
#

Let \(S\) be a commutative semigroup, \(X\) be a minimal \(S\)-system, and \(\pi : W \to X\) be its natural extension. We have that

\[ (\pi \times \pi )^{-1} \mathbf{RP}_X = \mathbf{RP}_W. \]

As a result, the map \(W/ \mathbf{RP}_W \to X / \mathbf{RP}_X\) given by

\[ [w]_{\mathbf{RP}_W} \mapsto [\pi w]_{\mathbf{RP}_X} \]

is an isomorphism of \(S\)-systems.

Proof ▶

That \(\mathbf{RP}_W \subseteq (\pi \times \pi )^{-1} \mathbf{RP}_X\) follows from lemma 107. To see that \((\pi \times \pi )^{-1} \mathbf{RP}_X \subseteq \mathbf{RP}_W\), let \((\varphi , \psi ) \in W^2\), and suppose \((\pi \varphi , \pi \psi ) \in \mathbf{RP}_X\). We must show that \((\varphi , \psi ) \in \mathbf{RP}_W\).

First, we claim that \((\varphi (g), \psi (g)) \in \mathbf{RP}_X\) for all \(g \in \gro (S)\). Indeed, let \(g \in \gro (S)\). By theorem 241, there exist \(s, t \in S\) such that \((\igro s)g = \igro (t)\). Since

\[ (\varphi (e_{\gro (S)}), \psi (e_{\gro (S)})) = (\pi \varphi , \pi \psi ) \in \mathbf{RP}_X, \]

by the \(S\)-invariance of \(\mathbf{RP}_X\) (see ??), we have that

\[ s(\varphi (g), \psi (g)) = (\varphi ((\igro s)g), \psi ((\igro s)g)) = t (\varphi (e_{\gro (S)}), \psi (e_{\gro (S)})) \in t\mathbf{RP}_X \subseteq \mathbf{RP}_X. \]

By theorem 227, \(s(\varphi (g), \psi (g)) \in \mathbf{RP}_X\) implies that \((\varphi (g), \psi (g)) \in \mathbf{RP}_X\), as desired.

Now we will show that \((\varphi , \psi ) \in \mathbf{RP}_W\). Let \(U, V \subseteq W\) and \(\alpha \subseteq W^2\) be open neighborhoods of \(\varphi \), \(\psi \), and \(\Delta _W\) respectively. By the definition of product topology on \(W \subseteq X^{\gro (S)}\), there exists a finite set \(F \subseteq \gro (S)\) and, for all \(f \in F\), open sets \(U_f, V_f \subseteq X, \alpha _f \subseteq X^2\) containing \(\varphi (f)\), \(\psi (f)\), and \(\Delta _X\) respectively such that

  • for \(\chi \in W\), if \(\chi (f) \in U_f\) for all \(f \in F\), then \(\chi \in U\),

  • for \(\chi \in W\), if \(\chi (f) \in V_f\) for all \(f \in F\), then \(\chi \in V\),

  • for \((\chi _1, \chi _2) \in W^2\), if \((\chi _1(f), \chi _2(f)) \in \alpha _f\) for all \(f \in F\), then \((\chi _1, \chi _2) \in \alpha \).

By theorem 241, there exists \(h \in \gro (S)\) and, for all \(f \in F\), \(s_f \in S\) such that \(f = (\igro s_f) h\). Then for all \(f \in F\), we have

\[ s_f \big(\varphi (h)\big) = \varphi ((\igro s_f) h) = \varphi (f) \in U_f. \]

It follows that \(\bigcap _{f \in F} s_f^{-1} U_f\) is an open neighborhood of \(\varphi (h)\). Similarly, \(\bigcap _{f \in F} s_f^{-1} V_f\) and \(\bigcap _{f \in F} s_f^{-1} \alpha _f\) are open neighborhoods of \(\psi (h)\) and \(\Delta _X\) respectively. By the first claim, \((\varphi (h), \psi (h)) \in \mathbf{RP}_X\), so there exists \(s \in S\) such that

\begin{align} \label{eqn_nonempty_subset_of_x2} \left( \bigcap _{f \in F} s_f^{-1} U_f \times \bigcap _{f \in F} s_f^{-1} V_f \right) \cap s^{-1} \bigcap _{f \in F} s_f^{-1} \alpha _f \neq \emptyset . \end{align}

Since \(\gro (S)\) acts on \(W\) by homeomorphisms and \(\pi \) is surjective, the map \(\pi \circ h: W \to X\) defined by \(\chi \mapsto \chi (h)\) is surjective. Hence there exists \((\eta , \rho ) \in W^2\) such that \((\eta (h), \rho (h))\) belongs to the set in ??. We see that for all \(f \in F\),

\[ (\eta (f), \rho (f)) = s_f (\eta (h), \rho (h)) \in U_f \times V_f, \]

whereby \((\eta ,\rho ) \in U \times V\). Moreover, for all \(f \in F\),

\[ s (\eta (f), \rho (f)) = s s_f (\eta (h), \rho (h)) \in \alpha _f, \]

whereby \((\eta , \rho ) \in s^{-1} \alpha \). Therefore,

\[ (\eta , \rho ) \in (U \times V) \cap s^{-1} \alpha , \]

showing that \((\varphi ,\psi ) \in \mathbf{RP}_W\), as was to be shown.

Finally, we will show that the map \(W/ \mathbf{RP}_W \to X / \mathbf{RP}_X\) given by

\begin{align} \label{eqn_factor_quotient_map_def} [w]_{\mathbf{RP}_W} \mapsto [\pi w]_{\mathbf{RP}_X} \end{align}

is an isomorphism of \(S\)-systems. Since \((\pi \times \pi ) \mathbf{RP}_W \subseteq \mathbf{RP}_X\), the surjective composition \(W \to X \to X/\mathbf{RP}_X\) descends to the map \(W / \mathbf{RP}_W \to X / \mathbf{RP}_X\) defined in ??, showing that the map in ?? is well-defined, continuous (by a defining characteristic of the quotient topology), and surjective. It intertwines the actions of \(S\) since \(\mathbf{RP}_W\) and \(\mathbf{RP}_X\) are \(S\)-invariant.

We only have left to show that \(\pi \) is injective. Suppose \(w, z \in W\) so that \([\pi w]_{\mathbf{RP}_X} = [\pi z]_{\mathbf{RP}_X}\). Then \((\pi \times \pi ) (w,z) \in \mathbf{RP}_X\). Since \((\pi \times \pi )^{-1} \mathbf{RP}_X = \mathbf{RP}_W\), we have that \((w,z) \in \mathbf{RP}_W\), whereby \([w]_{\mathbf{RP}_W} = [z]_{\mathbf{RP}_W}\), as was to be shown.

Theorem 250
#

Let \(S\) be a commutative semigroup, \(X\) be a minimal \(S\)-system, and \(\pi : W \to X\) be its natural extension. The following \(S\)-systems are the same up to isomorphism:

  1. \(X/\mathbf{RP}_{X,S}\), the largest equicontinuous factor of the \(S\)-system \(X\);

  2. \(W / \mathbf{RP}_{W,S}\), the largest equicontinuous factor of the natural extension \(W\) of \(X\) as an \(S\)-system; and

  3. \(W / \mathbf{RP}_{W,\gro (S)}\), the largest equicontinuous factor of the natural extension \(W\) of \(X\) as a \(\gro (S)\)-system, made into an \(S\)-system via the homomorphism \(\igro : S \to \gro (S)\).

Proof of theorem 250 ▶

That 1 is isomorphic to 2 follows from theorem 249.

To see that 2 is isomorphic to 3, note first that since \(X\) is a minimal \(S\)-system, so is \(W\) (by theorem 247). It follows that \(W\) is a minimal \(\gro (S)\)-system. Since the actions of \(S\) and \(\gro (S)\) commute, theorem 240 gives that \(\mathbf{RP}_{W,S} = \mathbf{RP}_{W,\gro (S)}\). Therefore, as sets \(W / \mathbf{RP}_{W,S} = W / \mathbf{RP}_{W,\gro (S)}\). We need only show that \(S\) acts on this set in the same way in both descriptions. But this is immediate since both actions are induced from the action of \(S\) on \(W\).
